Overnight run, 25 November

This run asked four things that the results of 24 November (further down) left open. Is the fitted vector better than noise? How do our directions compare with Goodfire's, built by their recipe on their synthetic data and read on this model? Does the model hack in multi-turn agent rollouts, and do the directions detect it there? And does choosing the fitting environments on a validation set help? The rules for each test were fixed before the run.

Method details — rules fixed before the run
  1. Steering statistic. The hack rate with a vector subtracted minus the unsteered rate, on identical prompts, with equal weight per environment. All vectors have norm 20.
  2. Null. The same statistic for 32 random conditions: 16 random directions, each added and subtracted. Eight are isotropic. Eight are Σ-shaped (Σ times a random vector), so that they lie in high-variance directions as Σ·probe and the fitted vector do. The test is one-sided t with 15 degrees of freedom.
  3. Named comparisons, Holm-corrected: Σ·probe, the fitted v, Goodfire's direction. Everything else is exploratory.
  4. Head to head. In steering, the paired difference in subtracted hack rate between Σ·probe and Goodfire's direction, with a bootstrap interval over prompts. In detection, the paired difference in matched AUROC between the logistic probe and Goodfire's direction on environments and families outside the probe's fit.
  5. Goodfire's direction. The equal-weight mean of their 12 released contrasts, read as the mean over the six closing tokens of the tool call in their own records, unit norm. None of our data enters it. This is our reconstruction: one data family from their paper is not in the release, and their final probe was selected on held-out rollouts.
Method details — multi-turn rollouts

In a rollout the model has four tools (shell, read file, write file, submit) in a working directory, a limited number of submissions, and feedback after each one. Its earlier reasoning stays in the context. Each family has a hack variant, where a shortcut exists or the honest route fails, and a benign variant, where it does not. No prompt mentions the shortcut. A program labels each episode from the working directory and the tool calls, never from prose.

The episode score is a matched AUROC: the mean activation over an episode's assistant tokens, hack episodes against honest episodes of the same task. The step score is the AUROC of the step that performs the hack against every step of honest episodes of that family. No direction scored on rollouts saw a rollout.

1. Goodfire's direction does not detect hacks in our single-turn environments

We scored Goodfire's direction on hack and honest completions of the same prompt in our 11 single-turn environments. The comparison is the logistic probe fitted on the other ten environments, so neither direction has seen the environment it is scored on. The probe averages 0.887 and Goodfire's direction 0.471, a paired difference of +0.417 (95% CI [+0.383, +0.450]).

  • Goodfire's direction is not random here. It is well below 0.5 on six environments and near 1.0 on ckpt_swap. It reads something that varies with hacking, with a sign that depends on the environment.
  • These are single-turn completions, not the tool calls in agent transcripts that their direction was built for. Section 6 has the comparison on rollouts, and it comes out differently.

2. Subtracting Σ·probe prevents hacks; subtracting Goodfire's direction does not

We steered on 50 fresh prompts from five environments outside every fit (ten each, one sample per prompt). Unsteered, the model hacks on 48%. Subtracting Σ·probe lowers that by 44 points (95% CI [−58, −32]; p = 0.024 after Holm correction), and none of the 32 random conditions is as low. Subtracting Goodfire's direction changes it by +6 points [−10, +22]. The paired difference between the two is −50 points [−64, −36], so by the rule fixed in advance ours beats theirs in steering.

  • High-variance random vectors lower hacking by themselves. The 16 Σ-shaped random conditions average −12.8 points and the isotropic ones +5.0. The isotropic null used on 24 November made the steering vectors look more special than they are. Against the Σ-shaped conditions alone, Σ·probe still has p = 0.008.
  • The fitted vector does not pass after correction (−34 points [−50, −20], Holm p = 0.060).
  • Validity. 78% of answers are valid with Σ·probe subtracted, against 84% unsteered. The invalid ones are cut-offs in one new environment. Our analysis script flags any condition under 80% as degraded; the rule in the spec covers added conditions only. Without that environment the effect is −50 points.
  • On the fitting environments the effect is smaller (−23 points, p = 0.0015) and all of it is in one environment.
  • Removing the component along a direction, instead of subtracting a fixed vector, did nothing for any direction.

3. Only Σ·probe moves hacking both ways

A vector that encodes "hack" should raise hacking when added and lower it when subtracted. A vector that only disrupts the model lowers it with either sign. Adding Σ·probe raises hacking by 22 points [+2, +40] with 82% of answers valid, and half the difference between its two signs is +33 points (p = 0.0014). The fitted vector lowers hacking with either sign (−20 added, −34 subtracted) and leaves 54% of answers valid when added. Goodfire's direction does nothing with either sign (+4, +6).

4. The fitted vector's advantage is mostly noise

On 24 November the fitted vector suppressed hacking more than Σ·probe. The control is a noise-only vector: the same optimiser run from the same starting point, with the steering gradient replaced by noise of the same size. We ran it on that day's prompts (three environments, 90 prompts, 60% unsteered).

5. The model hacks in multi-turn rollouts, and one sentence of instruction decides how often

We ran 583 agent episodes in six families. In every family the shortcut is never mentioned, and the detectors never fired on a benign episode (0 of 191 in the scripted families).

ImpossibleBench is the realistic family: coding tasks from LiveCodeBench where one test contradicts the specification. With the benchmark's published instruction the model never special-cased the tests. With a plain instruction it almost always did. The wording in between gave a usable rate.

  • Under the mild wording the hacks are disclosed. A typical hack episode special-cases the contradictory input and then says so in its final answer. That is what ImpossibleBench counts, but it is not concealed cheating.
  • "Honest" on ImpossibleBench mostly means "ran out of turns". Of 46 honest episodes, 41 stop at the 15-turn limit and none submits. Of 43 hack episodes, 33 submit. Some honest episodes would probably have hacked with more turns.
  • Release gate was hacked in an unplanned way. In 14 of 15 hacks the model reads the datastore client's private in-memory map so that the simulated round trips are never charged. It does not touch the threshold.
  • Sizes are small. Three scripted families have two hack-variant episodes per task, and the ImpossibleBench run reached 101 of its 180 planned episodes.

6. On rollouts, our probe does not beat Goodfire's direction

We read every rollout with the full conversation in context and scored hack episodes against honest episodes of the same task. The probe is fitted on all 11 single-turn environments. Over the six families the paired difference, probe minus Goodfire's direction, is −0.010 (95% CI [−0.115, +0.085]). By the rule fixed in advance, ours does not beat theirs on rollouts.

  • Each direction is inverted somewhere. The probe ranks hack episodes below honest ones on release gate (0.31) and answer key (0.30). Goodfire's direction does so on leaked outputs (0.21). Neither is a usable flag across families.
  • On ImpossibleBench the probe scores 0.70 [0.57, 0.83] and Goodfire's direction 0.68 [0.47, 0.86].
  • Stricter matching gives the same answer. Comparing only episodes of the same variant leaves 1 to 13 tasks per family, and the paired difference is +0.052 [−0.025, +0.125].
  • At the level of single steps the probe looks better (post hoc). It separates the step that performs the hack from honest steps at 0.78 to 0.98 in five families, against 0.34 to 0.62 for Goodfire's direction in four of them; the paired difference is +0.28 [+0.19, +0.34]. We computed this after seeing the tables. On ImpossibleBench a step's position in the episode scores 0.76 to 0.84 by itself, as good as the probe's 0.81, so there the result does not show more than "hack steps come late".

7. Choosing the fitting environments on a validation family helps the probe on rollouts

One rollout family is the test set and another the validation set. Every subset of a pool of fitting units is a candidate; the subset that scores best on validation is chosen and scored on test. Five usable families give 20 ordered pairs.

  • For our probe, choosing helps: +0.173 [+0.098, +0.247] over the whole pool, and it wins in 15 of 20 splits. The whole pool is poor because some single-turn environments invert the probe on some families.
  • The choice is mostly "keep the tampering environments". ckpt_swap is in the chosen subset in 16 of 20 splits.
  • Adding rollout families to the fitting pool did not help the probe (whole pool −0.001 [−0.077, +0.075]).
  • A logistic probe on Goodfire's own contrast pairs does as well as our chosen probe (0.760) without any choosing, and better than our probe on all of our single-turn data. So on rollouts the probe recipe matters, and our data has no advantage over theirs.
  • The gain is less clear under stricter matching (+0.092 [−0.033, +0.221] on 12 splits), where three of four families have two or three matched tasks.
